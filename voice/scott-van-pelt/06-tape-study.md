# Scott Van Pelt — 06 Tape Study

*Eight segments, 2015–2024, broken down by how they're built. Researched Oct 23, 2026. This is
Scott Van Pelvis's register all year. Every description is a paraphrase and every example in
this folder is an original composition. Study the shape and never borrow a line.*

**How this was built:** from detailed recaps, reviews, network features and interviews. There
were no transcripts and no video. Beat timings below are estimates from reported segment
lengths. Links are at the bottom.

## The segments

### T1. Bad Beats — Boise State at San José State, Nov 2024
The marquee beat at the end of a block that ran past twelve minutes. San José was getting
about two touchdowns.
1. **Comfort.** San José's backers were covering by the hook, late.
2. **First kill.** A long pick-six with under two minutes left. Most beats end here.
3. **Resurrection.** There's still time. San José drives to the one inside the final minute,
   and a touchdown would mean a backdoor cover. Hope comes back, which is worse.
4. **The goal-line spiral.** Each failure narrated faster than the last: the run stuffed, the
   short throw short, the clock bleeding, the offense unable to get set.
5. **The lost thread.** He wonders aloud whether the quarterback was just sacked. The narrator
   is as lost as the bettor.
6. **Detonation.** One all-caps line, aimed at his co-host, not the camera.
7. **Collapse.** Head on the desk, laughing. He says he's sweating.
8. **Coda.** His own picks still went fine.
- **Steal:** two daggers, the second one a non-event; ten calm minutes banked before one
  scream; the scream goes to a person; a physical admission; a tiny self-serving footnote that
  deflates everything.

### T2. Bad Beats as a machine — Abilene Christian at Virginia, Nov 2020
No play detail survives; the shape of the block does.
- **Running order:** the marquee beat sits in the back third. With T1, that's two cases of the
  best one landing late.
- **Sourcing:** viewers submit beats; staff compile half an hour of them over an afternoon and
  trim. The hosts give each clip one silent look, mainly to get the names right, because it's
  those people's one time on TV. They never rehearse the call.
- **Origin:** reading recaps aloud fell flat on radio. What worked was callers asking whether
  the hosts had seen some obscure game.
- **The thesis behind it:** people remember their worst beats longer than their best wins.
- **Steal:** save the worst for last, get every name right, and never rehearse the reaction.

### T3. A closing essay — his father, 31 years on, Jan 2019
He called it the most personal piece he'd written for the show — composed, not ad-libbed.
1. **A habit as the opener.** He usually marks the birthday and sidesteps the day of the death.
   This year he couldn't.
2. **The arithmetic.** He counts the years and sets his life against his father's.
3. **The inventory.** What he'd say if he had a few minutes: how it all went, the
   grandchildren.
4. **The widening circle.** A friend buried her father that same day; another was traveling to
   a funeral.
5. **Patient, not doctor.** He repeats the advice he gives friends — don't bury grief — and
   admits he needs to take it himself.
6. **The permission slip.** He names the self-indulgence objection and answers it: everyone
   carries something, and this might nudge you to tell a living person how you feel. Then he
   concedes it might help no one.
7. **The close.** He misses him and hopes he'd be proud.
- **Steal:** the turn is a confession, not a thesis. The objection is answered before anyone
  raises it. The circle widens to the audience and narrows back to one man. Nothing follows it.

### T4. A closing essay — a eulogy for his dog, Apr 2022
- It ran after the first round of the NFL Draft — the biggest football night of the spring,
  given to a dog.
- It was specific about what made this one dog different, and about how much the dog gave.
- He came close to breaking down, and didn't.
- It became his most-viewed piece. He calls that kind of vulnerability deliberate.
- **Steal:** the size of the night makes the smallness of the subject land.

### T5. With a late colleague's daughters, June 2024
A guest conversation, ten years after their father's famous awards-show speech.
1. **Business first:** the cancer fund and a donor's matching gift.
2. **The personal turn:** what their father would think of the women they've become. He cried.
3. **The smallest close:** it was good to see them.
- **Steal:** the errand anchors the emotion. He voices the absent man's pride. The biggest
  feeling gets the plainest sentence.

### T6. His last radio show, June 2015
1. **The format bends:** his usual segment, renamed for the exit.
2. **Luck and intimacy:** how lucky he'd been, and how personal radio is.
3. **The life ledger:** the run measured in life events — bachelor, husband, father — and in
   people lost along the way.
4. **The chores list:** listeners at work, driving, doing the dishes, letting the hosts keep
   them company.
5. **The hand-off:** he thanks the staff and gives the slot to his partner.
- **Steal:** time measured in lives, not seasons; the audience pictured at its most ordinary;
  the last act generous.

### T7. The closing essay's range, season one, 2015
His word for the slot was "chameleon." It ran from a 74-year-old coach reviving a high-school
program (his favorite), to a pointed redirect of coverage that had fixated on a star's reality
TV, to a bald man's essay on a golfer's receding hairline. His standard was a real point every
night, never a half-effort.
- **Steal:** a small story carrying a big metaphor; a redirect of what everyone else is
  looking at; self-mockery by proxy.

### T8. Picks (thin)
No source described a specific picks episode. What's documented: the old radio show had a
picks bit with its own music; his co-host's on-air job is to feed setups and needle him; and he
reads out his own record as a coda, even mid-meltdown.

## Patterns across the tape

1. **Two textures.** Live narration is present tense, fragments, self-interruption and
   questions. The composed essay is whole sentences, a past-tense setup and one idea.
2. **The calm is the setup.** The one scream works because of the minutes of quiet before it.
3. **The scream goes to a person.** Never to the camera.
4. **He's the guy sweating in the next seat,** not the sharp. Expertise belongs to someone else;
   he carries the suffering.
5. **The people in the clips are guests.** Their names get checked. The roast comes from the
   narrated facts, not from insults.
6. **Sincerity is contained.** Its own slot, last, specific and composed, no jokes inside it,
   and nothing after it. It lands because it's contained, not because it's rare.

## Corrections to the older files in this folder

- **"Serious for a second" and "Okay. Back to it."** No source supports either. The documented
  flag is structural: the sincere piece gets its own slot, in the last position, and for a real
  loss there's no snap back. The guide's card still says sincerity is flagged out loud, so the
  league does both — the flag line and its own slot. Keep the snap-back for small asides only.
- **"That's a choice," "You've won. You've WON," "That's it. That's the thing," "Never bet.
  Ever":** none appear in the sixteen sources. The guide's Van Pelt card still lists "That's a
  choice" as his tell, and the guide outranks this folder, so they stay as the league's Van Pelt
  dialect — rationed to one a piece (`07-phrasebook.md`), not required in every piece as the old
  checklist had it. Jason may want the card updated.
- **"Enjoy the games, fellas"** is out entirely: it's Drew Magary's real sign-off with one word
  changed.
- **"Doesn't write in paragraphs" (`03`):** true of live narration, false of the composed essay,
  which uses full sentences.
- **The mock-moral as the Bad Beat's close (`02`, `05`):** the documented landing is the
  collapse plus a self-serving coda.
- **The colleague's daughters as a closing essay (`05`):** it was a guest conversation.
- **The co-host as "designated groaner" (`02`, `04`):** his documented role is instigator,
  needler and laughing witness.
- **"The Best Thing I Saw Today" cold open (`02`):** unconfirmed. Treat it as a league invention.
- **"Segments unchanged for a decade" (`01`):** out of date; Bad Beats grew into a long live
  segment that stretches or squeezes.
- **Length of the essay:** under 250 words, per the guide's card — grief pieces included.
- **His segment names as headers:** homage only, used deliberately. Never the words of his
  famous scream.

## For Scott Van Pelvis — the reset comes first

- **No clock narration, no play-by-play.** Reset rule 1 bans clock-time narration and
  decimal-by-decimal play-by-play, and `voice/USED.md` retires opening on a clock time and
  narrating the win-probability tracker. A Bad Beat gets one phrase of game time ("under a
  minute"), three or four beats, and no countdown.
- **Numbers are receipts, in words or numerals.** One a paragraph, two at the outside; the final
  margin once — "less than a point" beats the decimals. Instagram captions have their own
  budgets in the guide.
- **Nothing follows the dagger** (the guide). Van Pelt's real landing adds a physical admission
  and a coda after the scream; the league's version stops at the caps line.
- **The Bad Beat told to Zack** is an occasional callback, never two weeks running
  (`voice/USED.md`). The caps line can go to someone else.
- **Real-life moments** are the hardest thing he writes and the easiest to get wrong. Only what
  the member shared in the thread himself, at the level he shared it, never anyone's health,
  and the league's never list holds. Jason assigns them; Van Pelvis doesn't go looking.
